🧩 Context - How placements work on iGraal

iGraal's website surfaces promotional placements - banners, spots, and slots - for retailer partners, live across 5 country domains. Behind the scenes, an internal ops team commissions and schedules these placements through the PMT (Promotional Management Tool): choosing which retailer appears where, on which domain, and for how long, sometimes for a single day, sometimes for a multi-day campaign.

Setting a promotional slot for a retailer in the PMT

The same placement, live on the iGraal site

🚩 The problem

As a designer on the promotional placements team, I worked across both sides of this system: the placements shoppers see, and the PMT, the internal space ops colleagues rely on to schedule them. The team was already investing in automation, moving slot creation from a fully manual ops task toward an automated one. But automation introduced three problems no one had designed for yet:

  •  ⚠️ Error slots: when automation pulled data to create a slot but some data was missing, ops had no clear way to see it.
  •  🔀 Unclear slot origin: ops couldn't tell whether a slot was created manually, by the Salesforce automation, or by an internal tool that auto-filled empty slots with randomized placements to avoid blank displays on the site.
  •  🎨 No design attention, no feedback loop: these screens had never been reviewed for usability, despite being used daily by dozens of colleagues across five markets.

The original PMT view, randomized slot colors carried no information

🎯 Business goal

Reduce manual work for the ops team by automating slot creation, without losing the transparency and control ops relied on to trust and manage the system day to day, across all five domains.


🏃‍♀️ Joining the project

I joined the automation initiative at the point where the system could already create slots automatically, but the team needed to figure out how to make that automation legible to the ops colleagues who still had to review, trust, and occasionally fix it. For most of this phase, the project ran without a dedicated Product Manager, so I worked directly with the Engineering Manager and engineers to scope the problem, prioritize, and keep the project moving.


⛰️ My challenge

The core challenge was designing for trust in an automated system: ops colleagues needed to instantly understand what the system did on their behalf, why, and whether it needed their attention, without falling back to manually checking every slot, which would defeat the purpose of automating in the first place. Doing this without a PM in the room for most of the project also meant I had a bigger hand in shaping scope and priorities than usual.

🔎 Discovery & research

I started with a scheduling/slot-planning exploration, gathering inspiration and best practices from other B2B and complex-data products on how they visualize statuses, errors, and system-vs-manual actions in dense scheduling views.

🤖 Designing with AI

I used AI throughout this project to move faster without cutting corners on rigor: for ideating on design directions early on, and for prototyping in Figma Make, which let me build a testable, realistic prototype quickly enough to get it in front of ops colleagues within the project's tight timeline.

💡 Key insight

Ops colleagues didn't just need to know a slot had an error - they needed to know where it came from to know how to act on it. A manually created error meant "go fix the data." An auto-fill error meant something else entirely. Source and status together, not status alone, was what determined the right next action.


📝 Validating with users

I built the test in Useberry and ran 2 usability testing sessions with 3 ops colleagues in total on the Figma Make prototype. Each session opened with warm-up questions to understand their overall workflow and current use of the PMT, then moved into 2 structured tasks.

I also invited engineers to observe - 2 of the 4 were actively involved day-to-day - so they could hear directly from ops users and ask their own technical questions, closing the gap between what the team was building and what ops actually needed.

All 3 participants successfully completed both tasks - including finding and identifying the error slot - and all 3 rated the experience 7/7 for ease of use, Useberry's top score. The sessions also surfaced a concrete opportunity: using distinct, meaningful colors per slot source instead of the current randomized coloring, so ops could scan the calendar and immediately tell what was automated versus manual. Every participant called this out as a faster way to get an overview.

The final design gives ops colleagues instant clarity on both the status and the source of every promotional slot, turning an opaque automation into a system they can scan, trust, and act on quickly, across all 5 domains.

📈 The outcome

The redesigned PMT shipped alongside the automation work. I moved to a new team shortly after launch, so I don't have longitudinal usage data or post-launch quotes to point to, but the design decisions were grounded in direct evidence from usability testing beforehand: all 3 ops participants completed both tasks successfully, including locating the error slot, rated the experience 7/7 for ease of use, and confirmed the source-based color coding gave them a faster overview - surfacing a real need (faster validation) that got addressed before launch, not after.

The strongest signal of success here isn't a metric - it's that the team shipped a design validated by the people who use it daily, across 5 country domains, informed by direct conversation rather than assumption.

Key takeaways:
  •  I research and test designs directly with the users who'll rely on them daily
  •  I use AI deliberately to accelerate my workflow - from ideation to prototyping - without skipping validation
  •  Small, well-targeted design changes measurably improved how ops colleagues manage commercial slots and resolve errors, even without a large redesign

🌱 Key learning

Designing for automation isn't just about hiding manual work - it's about making the system's decisions visible enough that people still trust it. Running this project largely without a PM taught me to own scoping and stakeholder alignment more directly, and pairing AI-assisted prototyping with real usability testing let me move fast in early stages while still grounding decisions in direct user feedback.
